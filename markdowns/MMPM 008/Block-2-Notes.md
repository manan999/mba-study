## Unit 4: Rural Buying Behaviour and Influencing Factors

### 1. The Consumer Buying Decision Process
The buying process in rural India is highly **collective and community-validated** compared to individualistic urban buying patterns:

1. **Problem Recognition:** Guide by utility and functional needs rather than luxury. Marks use demos (e.g. Asian Paints demonstrating *Utsav* paint on post office walls to resolve peeling issues) to trigger need recognition.
2. **Information Search:** Relies heavily on **personal sources** (family, Sarpanch, village pradhan) and **experiential sources** (product touch-and-feel, trial packs, haat visits) rather than commercial ads.
3. **Evaluation of Alternatives:** Rural consumers weigh price, quality, and usage simplicity. Long evaluation cycles for high-involvement durables (tractors, TVs); quick cycles for daily convenience goods.
4. **Purchase Decision:** Influenced by perceived risk, attitudes of others, and cash availability. Final decision is often made collectively or in consultation with opinion leaders.
5. **Post-purchase Behaviour:** Positive experience builds strong brand loyalty. Dissatisfaction leads to negative word-of-mouth (WOM), which spreads rapidly in close-knit village networks.

---

### 2. Social Class and the SEC Grid
Defining social class in rural areas is challenging due to multiple occupations and agricultural seasonal cycles. Marketers use the **Socio-Economic Classification (SEC)** developed by the Market Research Society of India (MRSI):

* **Based on two criteria:**
  1. Education level of the chief wage earner.
  2. Type of house (Pucca, Semi-Pucca, or Kachha).
* **Classification Scale:** Ranging from **R1 to R4**.
  * **R1 & R2:** Higher consuming classes (e.g., graduate/higher secondary educated earning members in a pucca house). They buy high-involvement durables and make bulk purchases from nearby cities.
  * **R3 & R4:** Lower consuming, poorer families. They rely on local kiranas and weekly haats.

---

### 3. Role of Reference Groups and Opinion Leaders
Reference groups shape attitudes, introduce innovations, and validate purchase choices:

* **Opinion Leaders:** Traditional figures like the village Sarpanch, Panchayat members, school headmasters, or returning migrants. Educated youth are emerging as tech opinion leaders for durables (smartphones, bikes).
* **Marketer Applied Examples:**
  * **Hero Honda:** Recruited local village sales executives under the *"Har Gaaon Har Aangan"* campaign. They didn't pitch aggressively but engaged in informal chats to build trust.
  * **Tata Shakti:** Used local *Mistris* (masons) as opinion leaders to recommend roof sheets, leading consumers to demand *"63 no. wala chaddar"*.
  * **HUL:** Enlisted *Shakti Ammas* (village women entrepreneurs) as trusted local opinion leaders for selling hygiene and household products.
  * **Colgate:** Partnered with school teachers in the *"Bright Smiles, Bright Futures"* oral care campaign.

## Unit 5: Trends in Consumer Behaviour

### 1. Financial Inclusion & Community Empowerment
* **Financial Inclusion:** The drive for Jan Dhan Accounts (PMJDY) and accident insurance (PMSBY) has increased financial literacy and placed direct cash transfers (DBT) into rural pockets, boosting spending power.
* **Community Empowerment:** 50% reservation for women in Panchayats and the rise of **Self-Help Groups (SHGs)** have given women financial autonomy and a stronger voice in family purchasing decisions.

### 2. Trends in Rural Consumption Patterns
* **"Individualized Joint Families":** Families live in the same house but maintain separate kitchens to avoid conflict. This drives a shift from single large-pack purchases to multiple smaller-unit purchases of the same product.
* **Aspirational Spending:** Expenditure is rising in miscellaneous categories—particularly children's education, healthcare, two-wheelers, and digital services.
* **Brand Shift:** Increased demand for brands (e.g., Royal Enfield, LG, Samsung) due to television exposure and mobile penetration.

## Unit 6: Rural Marketing Research

### 1. Qualitative Approach vs. Survey Method
Quantitative survey questionnaires fail in rural areas due to low literacy levels, linguistic diversity, and respondent hesitation with formal scales. **Qualitative and observational research** is more suitable because it captures non-verbal cues, respects cultural hierarchies, and builds trust.

---

### 2. Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA)
A highly collaborative social research method that actively involves villagers in data collection:

* **Transect Walk:** Researcher walks through the village observing daily activities, facilities, and habits.
* **Village Mapping:** Villagers draw a map of their village resources on a blackboard, ground, or paper using colors or powders.
* **Time Line:** Villagers prepare a chronology of historical events to track community development.
* **Seasonality Diagram:** Maps data (income, illness, water scarcity) by calendar seasons.
* **Matrix Ranking:** Villagers rank preferences or attributes using stones, seeds, or symbols.
* **Venn Diagrams (Chapattis):** Uses varying-sized circles to rank the importance of institutions or individuals.

---

### 3. Key Qualitative Research Techniques
* **Participant Observation:** Researcher lives in the village, participating in rituals to understand shared cultural meanings.
* **Projective Visual Tools:** Using cartoons, caricatures, or **Thematic Apperception Tests (TAT)** rather than detailed photos, as clean visual illustrations reduce information processing limits.
* **Scale Simplification:** Replacing 5 or 7-point Likert scales with simple **3-point pictorial scales** (e.g., happy/neutral/sad faces) that require lower spatial processing capability.

## Unit 7: Mobile Phone Industry Case Study

* **Growth Drivers:** Dropping handset costs and ultra-cheap data plans triggered rapid mobile adoption.
* **Behavioral Shifts:** Mobile phones transitioned from luxury products to core utility assets (handling banking, weather forecasting, crop sales, and entertainment).
* **Purchase Influences:** Rural youth and migrant workers act as prime influencers in handset brand choices.

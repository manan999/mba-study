### UNIT 4: PERCEPTIONS

#### 1. Concept of Perception vs. Sensation
*   **Sensation:** The immediate and direct response of sensory receptors (eyes, ears, nose, mouth, skin) to basic stimuli (light, color, sound, odor, texture). It is raw, unprocessed sensory input.
*   **Perception:** The process by which individuals select, organize, and interpret these raw sensations to form a meaningful, coherent picture of the world. It is highly subjective, meaning reality is defined by a consumer's internal interpretation rather than objective physical metrics.
*   **Stages of Perception:**
    1.  *Primitive Categorization:* Grouping basic features of a stimulus (e.g., assuming a yellow bottle with a lemon picture is lemon juice).
    2.  *Cue Check:* Analyzing specific cues (e.g., comparing packaging shape, colors, and layout).
    3.  *Confirmation Check:* Matching the cues to a mental schema (e.g., confirming the product fits the "beverage" schema).
    4.  *Confirmation Completion:* Finalizing the schema selection and acting upon it (e.g., drinking the liquid).

#### 2. Sensory Thresholds and Weber's Law
*   **Absolute Threshold:** The minimum level of stimulus intensity required for a sensory channel to detect a sensation. If a stimulus is below this threshold, it is not noticed (e.g., billboard print that is too small for motorists to read).
*   **Differential Threshold:** The ability of a sensory system to detect changes or differences between two stimuli.
*   **Just Noticeable Difference (JND) & Weber's Law:**
    *   *JND:* The minimum change in a stimulus that can be detected.
    *   *Weber's Law:* The stronger the initial stimulus, the greater the change must be for it to be noticed.
    *   *Formula:* $\frac{\Delta I}{I} = K$ (where $I$ is original intensity, $\Delta I$ is change, and $K$ is a constant).
    *   *Marketing Applications:*
        *   *When keeping things similar:* When updating logos, characters, or packaging, changes should be *below* JND so consumers do not lose familiarity (e.g., subtle, gradual packaging updates by Pepsi or Cadbury).
        *   *When emphasizing changes:* Price discounts, product size increases, or quality improvements should be *above* JND so they are noticed and drive sales.

#### 3. Subliminal Perception: Does it Work?
*   **Definition:** The processing of stimuli that are below the absolute threshold level (ATL) of conscious awareness. Literally means "below threshold."
*   **Subliminal Techniques:**
    *   *Embeds:* Tiny, hidden figures (often sexual/symbolic) airbrushed into ads.
    *   *Auditory Messages:* Sub-audible messages or backmasking (backwards recordings) in self-help tracks.
*   **Does it work?** No, there is no scientific evidence that subliminal advertising influences consumer purchase behaviour in real-world settings.
    *   *Why it fails:*
        1.  *Individual Thresholds:* Absolute thresholds vary widely between individuals. What is subliminal to one is undetectable or fully visible to another.
        2.  *Attention Control:* Stimuli must be attended to for message comprehension. Subliminal inputs bypass the attentional gate.
        3.  *Action Control:* Subliminal cues are too weak to override conscious beliefs, values, or immediate goals.
    *   *Note:* Threshold messaging (played at barely audible levels) is not subliminal since it is above the threshold of detection.

#### 4. Case Study Applications: Perceptions
*   **Influence of Advertising on Perceptions:** Ads shape brand schemas. For example, *Patanjali Dant Kanti* shifted perceptions of toothpaste from cosmetic/refreshing to health-oriented/ayurvedic by using herb visual cues, traditional doctors, and clinical/nature associations.
*   **Extended Product Offerings (e.g., Raymond's MoU with Khadi Commission):** When a premium brand (Raymond) partners with a traditional craft brand (Khadi), it leverages the premium brand's quality and style associations to upgrade the traditional brand's perception from rustic/affordable to premium heritage wear, expanding the market to younger, style-conscious consumers.

### UNIT 5: LEARNING AND MEMORY

#### 1. Concept of Learning
*   **Definition:** A relatively permanent change in behaviour (or behavioural capability) resulting from direct or vicarious experience.
*   **Incidental Learning:** Casual, unintentional acquisition of knowledge (e.g., hummed advertising jingles for products a consumer does not buy).

#### 2. Theories of Learning
*   **Classical Conditioning (Ivan Pavlov):** Learning by association. A neutral stimulus (Conditioned Stimulus - CS) is paired with an Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS) that naturally elicits a response. Over time, the CS alone produces a similar response.
    *   *Stimulus Generalization:* Reacting to stimuli similar to the CS (e.g., buying look-alike "me-too" packaging).
    *   *Stimulus Discrimination:* Learning to distinguish between similar stimuli (e.g., established brands urging buyers to avoid cheap imitations).
*   **Operant / Instrumental Conditioning (B.F. Skinner):** Learning to perform behaviours that yield positive outcomes (rewards) and avoid those that yield negative outcomes (punishments).
    *   *Positive Reinforcement:* Reward strengthens a response (e.g., getting compliments on a perfume).
    *   *Negative Reinforcement:* Removing a negative stimulus strengthens a response (e.g., ads showing a shoe breaking in public, motivating the purchase of a durable brand to avoid embarrassment).
    *   *Extinction / Punishment:* Unpleasant consequences eliminate the behaviour.
    *   *Schedules of Reinforcement:*
        *   *Fixed-Interval:* Reward after a set time (e.g., seasonal clearance sales).
        *   *Variable-Interval:* Reward after varying times (e.g., surprise random pop-up discounts).
        *   *Fixed-Ratio:* Reward after a set number of responses (e.g., buy 5, get 1 free loyalty cards).
        *   *Variable-Ratio:* Reward after an unpredictable number of responses (e.g., slot machines, random scratch cards). Highly resistant to extinction.

#### 3. Memory Structure and Functioning
*   **Information-Processing Approach:** Memory has three distinct storage systems:
    1.  *Sensory Memory:* Very temporary storage (1-2 seconds) of immediate sensory inputs (e.g., smelling freshly brewed coffee). If attended to, it moves to STM.
    2.  *Short-Term Memory (STM):* Working memory that holds current information. Limited capacity (7±2 chunks) and brief duration (under 30 seconds).
    3.  *Long-Term Memory (LTM):* Permanent storage of unlimited capacity. Requires *elaborate rehearsal* (thinking about meaning, repetition) to transfer from STM to LTM.
*   **Relationship between STM and LTM:** STM holds active working thoughts. Repeat exposure and cognitive associations transfer info to LTM. Retrieval is the process of accessing stored brand schemas in LTM and bringing them back to active STM to guide choices.
*   **Marketer Strategy:** Marketers must target LTM for brand equity. However, to get there, they must design ad messages that respect the constraints of STM (avoiding cognitive overload by using simple brand names, catchy jingles, and chunked slogans like "Amul: The Taste of India").

### UNIT 6: ATTITUDE AND ATTITUDE CHANGE

#### 1. Concept of Consumer Attitude
*   **Definition:** A learned predisposition to behave in a consistently favorable or unfavorable way toward a given object (product, brand, service).
*   **Measurement Example:** Attitude toward men's skincare products can be measured using Likert scales (agree/disagree statements regarding benefits, e.g., "skincare is important for professional grooming") or Semantic Differential scales (opposite adjectives, e.g., "Greasy vs. Non-greasy", "Premium vs. Economy").

#### 2. The Behavioral Intentions (BI) Model (Theory of Reasoned Action)
Developed by Fishbein and Ajzen to predict behavioral intentions ($BI$) rather than just attitude toward the object:
$$B \approx BI = W_1(A_B) + W_2(SN)$$
*   **Components:**
    *   $B$: Actual Behavior.
    *   $BI$: Behavioral Intention (the best predictor of behavior).
    *   $A_B$: Attitude toward performing the specific behavior (e.g., attitude toward *buying* a sports car, not just the car itself). Calculated as:
        $$A_B = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i e_i$$
        (where $b_i$ is the belief that performing the behavior leads to consequence $i$, and $e_i$ is the evaluation of consequence $i$).
    *   $SN$: Subjective Norm (social pressure from key referents). Calculated as:
        $$SN = \sum_{j=1}^n NB_j MC_j$$
        (where $NB_j$ is the normative belief that referent $j$ thinks the consumer should/should not act, and $MC_j$ is the motivation to comply with referent $j$).
    *   $W_1, W_2$: Empirical weights representing the relative importance of personal attitudes vs. social pressure.

### UNIT 7: PERSONALITY AND SELF-CONCEPT

#### 1. Theories of Personality
*   **Freudian Psychoanalytic Theory:** Personality is a conflict among three systems:
    *   *Id:* Primitive, impulsive, pleasure-seeking drives (operates on immediate gratification).
    *   *Ego:* The conscious, rational control center (balances the Id and Superego).
    *   *Superego:* The internal moral code/conscience (strives for perfection and social conformity).
*   **Neo-Freudian Theories (e.g., Karen Horney's CAD Model):** Relies on social relationships:
    *   *Compliant (C):* Move toward others (seek love/approval). Prefer famous brand names.
    *   *Aggressive (A):* Move against others (seek power/success). Prefer status/premium brands to show superiority.
    *   *Detached (D):* Move away from others (seek independence). Prefer unique, non-conforming brands.
*   **Trait Theory:** Personality is measured by specific traits (e.g., innovativeness, materialism, self-consciousness).

#### 2. Self-Concept
*   Consumers buy products that align with their self-concept (how they perceive themselves). Marketers align product personality (e.g., Raymond's "The Complete Man") with the target consumer's aspirational self-concept.

### UNIT 8: CONSUMER MOTIVATION AND INVOLVEMENT

#### 1. Concept of Motivation
*   **Motivation:** The driving force within individuals that impels them to action. It is produced by a state of tension resulting from an unsatisfied need.
*   **Motives:** Undergrad reasons for behaviour. Can be:
    *   *Physiological:* Biological needs (hunger, thirst, shelter).
    *   *Psychogenic:* Psychological needs (status, power, affiliation, achievement).

#### 2. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs
Classifies human needs into five hierarchical levels:
1. Physiological $\to$ 2. Safety $\to$ 3. Social/Belongingness $\to$ 4. Ego/Esteem $\to$ 5. Self-Actualization.
*   **Critical Limitations:**
    *   *Strict Hierarchy is Unrealistic:* Higher needs can be active even if lower needs are unmet (e.g., fasting for religious self-actualization).
    *   *Multi-Need Fulfillment:* A single product can satisfy multiple levels simultaneously (e.g., dining out satisfies physiological hunger and social belonging).
    *   *Culture-Bound:* Reflects Western individualism. In collectivistic cultures (like India), group social needs often override individual esteem needs.

#### 3. Motivational Conflicts
1.  **Approach-Approach Conflict:** Choosing between two positive, desirable alternatives (e.g., choosing between two holiday packages). Marketers resolve this by adding unique features or temporary promotional incentives.
2.  **Approach-Avoidance Conflict:** A choice has both positive benefits and negative consequences (e.g., wanting tasty snacks but fearing weight gain). Marketers resolve this by offering guilt-free solutions (e.g., low-calorie snacks, diet soft drinks) to reduce *cognitive dissonance* (post-purchase doubt/tension).
3.  **Avoidance-Avoidance Conflict:** Choosing between two negative, undesirable alternatives (e.g., paying a heavy repair bill for an old appliance vs. spending on a new one). Marketers resolve this by offering easy payment EMI terms, high trade-in exchange value, or extended warranties.
